Sources for the generic broker margin progression

These sources motivate the financial concepts and system boundaries of the eight stages. The Agda modules supply their own constructive proofs. None of these sources supplies a postulate, a calibrated parameter, or a correctness theorem for this implementation. The progression has no concrete market scenario or instrument dependency.

Stage Method implemented here Relevant sources
01 Cash Signed replacement claim, available cash, and the distinction between requesting and receiving cash. BCBS FX, especially collateral arrangements and transfer timing.
02 Scenarios Coverage for every allowed state; supplied rational bounds support decisions without deciding arbitrary real order. Robust optimization for universal uncertainty constraints; BCBS FX for stressed exposure management.
03 Netting sets Separate authorized sets, individual coverage, and aggregate unsecured exposure. BCBS FX for enforceable netting and collateral agreements.
04 Collateral Inventory allocation, scenario-dependent eligible value, retention factors, and coverage-preserving substitution. BCBS FX and BCBS–IOSCO for collateral eligibility, valuation, and haircuts.
05 Journal Identified actual receipts, per-client balances, replay idempotency, and order-independent accounting. FINOS CDM for business event identity; Saltzer, Reed, and Clark for application-level duplicate suppression.
06 Revaluation Certified adverse claim and collateral changes; an additive reserve sufficient after actual receipt. Brigo–Pallavicini for margining and gap risk; robust optimization for bounds over supplied states.
07 Calls A minimum-transfer policy with a proved bound on the remaining unsecured amount. BCBS–IOSCO for the minimum-transfer concept; the precise policy is our definition.
08 Controller Composition of inventory, revaluation, transfer policy, and receipt accounting into conditional all-client guarantees. The preceding sources motivate the components; the composition and dependent certificate transport are proved here.

BCBS: FX settlement risk, 2013

Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, Supervisory guidance for managing risks associated with the settlement of foreign exchange transactions, February 2013, BCBS 241. Official publication, official PDF.

Sections 3.3.5–3.3.8 address enforceable netting agreements, collateral agreement terms, variation margin for the specified FX counterparties, and stressed collateral valuation with conservative haircuts. These motivate separate netting sets and explicitly supplied collateral values. Sections 3.2, 3.4, and 3.6 treat principal, liquidity, and legal risks separately: replacement-cost coverage alone does not certify payment settlement, currency funding, or enforceability. Sections 3.3.5–3.3.8, printed page 16.

The model receives the legally authorized grouping and available collateral as inputs. It does not determine applicable law, validate a collateral agreement, or implement the guidance's operational requirements.

BCBS–IOSCO: margin requirements, 2020

Basel Committee on Banking Supervision and International Organization of Securities Commissions, Margin requirements for non-centrally cleared derivatives, April 2020 revision. Official dated standard.

Requirement 2.3 discusses minimum transfer amounts; Element 4 addresses eligible collateral and haircuts. These support the general concepts used in Stages 04 and 07. No regulatory haircut schedule, monetary threshold, entity test, or model approval rule is encoded. The code's rule is explicit: request zero when the gap is at most the supplied minimum transfer amount, otherwise request the whole gap. Its equality boundary and residual-bound theorem are our formal choices. Requirements 2.1–2.3 and Element 4.

Requirement 1.1 excludes physically settled FX forwards and swaps from this document's margin requirements and directs their variation-margin treatment to supervisory guidance or national regulation, referring to BCBS 241. Thus this source provides general margin concepts, not an assertion that this generic controller is a regulatory implementation for FX swaps. Requirement 1.1.

Ben-Tal and Nemirovski: robust optimization, 1998

Aharon Ben-Tal and Arkadi Nemirovski, Robust Convex Optimization, Mathematics of Operations Research 23(4), 769–805, 1998. DOI: 10.1287/moor.23.4.769, author-hosted paper.

The paper's robust feasibility condition requires constraints to hold for every parameter in a specified uncertainty set. This supports the interpretation of Allowed and universal coverage certificates. Our state types need not be finite or convex; we prove implications from supplied bounds rather than implement the paper's optimization algorithms. Definition in Section 1.

The development does not infer an uncertainty set from data, prove that future markets stay inside it, or construct optimal real-valued bounds automatically. Conservative rational requirements can certify coverage without being least.

Brigo and Pallavicini: margining and gap risk, 2014

Damiano Brigo and Andrea Pallavicini, Nonlinear consistent valuation of CCP cleared or CSA bilateral trades with initial margins under credit, funding and wrong-way risks, Journal of Financial Engineering 1(1), 1450001, 1–60, 2014. Publisher and DOI: 10.1142/S2345768614500019.

The paper studies valuation with initial and variation margins, collateral, gap credit risk, funding, and wrong-way risk. It motivates treating adverse changes during the interval before collateral becomes available as an explicit part of the model. Stage 06 uses deterministic, universally quantified change bounds; its additive sufficient reserve is not the paper's stochastic valuation model or a calibrated initial-margin methodology. Publisher abstract.

The accessible 2013 FAQ, arXiv:1312.0128 by the same authors is a separate work, not an alternative bibliographic identity for the 2014 paper. The 2014 paper is also listed in Brigo's author-hosted 2017 presentation.

No default probability, dependence calibration, discounting equation, BSDE, or funding valuation adjustment is implemented in this progression. Conditional zero unsecured closeout loss does not eliminate these other risks.

FINOS: Common Domain Model event model

FINOS Common Domain Model, Event Model, official living documentation, accessed 8 September 2026. Official event model, Event Identifier.

The documentation distinguishes business events, workflow state, message information, and event identifiers. An event identifier supports references from other workflow steps; it is not merely the delivery message identifier. This motivates identifying the underlying receipt independently of a repeated delivery. Event and message identifiers.

Stage 05 is a small independent ledger model, not a CDM implementation. Its finite catalog fixes each event's owner and nonnegative amount before ingestion. A reused ID cannot carry a changed payload through this API. Distinct IDs must represent distinct actual receipts; corrections, reversals, identity assignment, and authentication require an additional model. The CDM citation does not establish those premises or prove the ledger's replay theorem.

Saltzer, Reed, and Clark: duplicate suppression, 1984

Jerome H. Saltzer, David P. Reed, and David D. Clark, End-to-End Arguments in System Design, ACM Transactions on Computer Systems 2(4), 277–288, November 1984. DOI: 10.1145/357401.357402, MIT-hosted author text.

The discussion of duplicate message suppression explains why application retries can create duplicates that a communication layer cannot recognize. Application knowledge is needed to identify the duplicated action. This motivates proving receipt identity and balance preservation at the journal layer. Duplicate message suppression, PDF page 6.

Our proofs concern pure ledger transitions: replay, permutation, and batches containing the same event IDs produce equal ledger states. They do not establish network exactly-once delivery, crash recovery, durable commits, concurrent-store linearizability, or the authenticity of a payment acknowledgement.

Scope of the composed result

Stage 08 combines proved bounds for supplied claims, collateral valuations, allowed states, and immutable actual-receipt data. Additional recorded cash must be distinct from cash already counted in the collateral allocation. Settlement of a computed plan is checked per client using newly credited receipts; a failed check means that the plan is unfulfilled, since a conservative plan need not be economically minimal.

After the required actual receipts, the controller bounds each client's residual by its supplied transfer tolerance, and the broker's aggregate residual and severity-weighted closeout loss by the sum of tolerances. Zero tolerance gives complete modeled coverage. Cubical paths additionally transport the full certificate across replay-equivalent ledger states. These are implementation theorems with explicit premises, not claims attributed to the cited literature.

This progression makes no claim of regulatory compliance, empirical adequacy, legal enforceability, settlement-principal protection, or native-currency liquidity sufficiency.
